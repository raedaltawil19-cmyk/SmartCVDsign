# Skill: credential_and_qualification_interpretation

Semantic interpretation before placement. Decide **what a fact is** before recommending where it belongs or how it should be phrased. Applies to any country, education system, profession and qualification type.

## Taxonomy

Distinguish, and never conflate:

- **Formal education** — a structured programme of study at an educational institution.
- **Academic degree** — the qualification awarded on completion of such a programme.
- **Certificate / licence** — a formal attestation issued after an examination or authorisation process, often by a professional or public body.
- **Course / training** — shorter or non-degree instruction, internal or external, with or without a certificate.
- **Professional experience** — work actually performed in a role, including responsibilities and results.
- **Skill / competency** — a capability the candidate possesses, however acquired.

## Placement rule

Place a fact according to **what the fact is**, not according to how relevant it feels. Relevance decides *whether* to mention something; its nature decides *where*.

One real-world episode can legitimately produce facts for two different sections. Training received belongs with education/training; the practical application of that training at work belongs with professional experience. In that case produce **two independent recommendations**, one per target, and link them only if one genuinely cannot be applied without the other.

## Interpretation rules

- **Never downgrade or reinterpret a qualification because its description is short.** A completed programme with a stated degree or credential is a completed qualification; a brief description is a wording matter at most, not an incomplete education.
- **Never treat a recognition, validation or assessment claim as equivalent to a domestic qualification.** A statement that a foreign qualification has been recognised or assessed says something about a process, not about the award of a domestic degree, licence or professional title.
- **When such a claim is genuinely ambiguous, narrow the recommendation to the ambiguity itself** — which body performed the assessment, and what kind of assessment it was — instead of questioning the qualification.
- **Never infer a professional title, authorisation or level** that the CV does not state.
- If the nature of a fact cannot be determined from the CV, say so and ask; do not guess a category.

## Illustrative examples (examples only — never universal rules)

- A foreign university programme listed with an awarded degree and the note that it has been validated in the candidate's new country: the qualification is complete. If anything is unclear, it is the *type of validation and the assessing body* — that alone is the recommendation.
- A short communication-technique course the candidate later used in client work: the course belongs to training; the client work belongs to experience.

These illustrate the reasoning. They are not statements about any particular programme, authority, course or employer.
